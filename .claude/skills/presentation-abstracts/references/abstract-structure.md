# The Four-Sentence Abstract Structure

Based on Kent Beck's approach (see [kent-beck-guidance.md](kent-beck-guidance.md)), aim for four key
sentences, flexibly applied:

1. **State the problem** — what challenge or gap exists?
2. **Why it matters** — why should the audience care about this problem?
3. **Your startling sentence** — the core insight, solution, or claim (this is the hook!)
4. **The implication** — what does this mean for the audience? What will they gain?

## Example

> **[Problem]** Many developers struggle with testing code that uses HttpClient.
> **[Why it matters]** Without proper testing, bugs slip through and refactoring becomes risky.
> **[Startling sentence]** You can fully mock HttpClient in three simple patterns without any external libraries.
> **[Implication]** Attendees will leave with reusable patterns they can apply immediately in their own projects.

## Finding Your One Startling Sentence

- Boil the entire talk down to ONE compelling statement
- It should make the reader's eyes open wide
- Be specific and concrete — vague claims are forgettable
- Don't be afraid to make a bold, falsifiable claim

## The Full Talk Structure (for context)

When the abstract needs to hint at depth, remember the talk itself should follow:

1. **Problem** — make the audience understand and believe it's important
2. **Solution** — your approach to solving it
3. **Defense** — evidence that it actually works
4. **Related work** — what makes your approach novel
