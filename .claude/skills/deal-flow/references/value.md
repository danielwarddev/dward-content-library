# 3. The Value Conversation

**Job:** understand the desired future state and what reaching it is worth.

The longest and most important conversation. Price is decided here — not in the Closing conversation.
Closing is where price is *stated*; Value is where it becomes justifiable.

## The Shift

| From | To |
| ---- | -- |
| What's wrong today | What "good" looks like in 6 months |
| What you'd deliver | What outcome they'd get |
| Hours, agendas, topics | Value, consequences, and cost of inaction |

If the buyer is comparing your workshop to another vendor's day rate, this conversation didn't happen.

## Questions Worth Asking

Their answers, in their words, become the proposal.

**Desired future state**
- "Six months from now, if this went well, what's different?"
- "How would you know it worked? What would you see?"
- "Who else would notice?"

**Quantifying**
- "How many developers are we talking about?"
- "What are the licenses costing you a year?"
- "If each of them got even an hour a week back, what's that worth here?"
- "What's the cost of a bad AI-generated change reaching production?"

**Cost of inaction**
- "What happens if nothing changes?"
- "Has anyone above you asked whether the licenses are paying off?"
- "Is there a date by which you need an answer on that?"

**Constraints**
- "How do purchases like this normally get approved?"
- "What's the rough range you'd expect for something like this?"
- "Anything that would stop this from happening even if you wanted it?"

## Anchoring

The math is usually easy and worth doing out loud, in their numbers:

> 15 developers × Copilot licenses ≈ several thousand dollars a year, plus 15 salaries. If the
> workshop moves even a fraction of that team from "fancy autocomplete" to genuinely productive,
> a $3,000 half-day is a rounding error against the license spend alone.

**Let them do the arithmetic where possible.** A number the buyer says out loud is far more durable
than one you assert.

## Signal the Price Range Here

Do not let price be a surprise at Closing. Late in this conversation, put the range in the room:

> "Engagements like this usually land in the $2,500–3,500 range. Does that fit how you'd budget it?"

This does two jobs: it disqualifies a buyer with no budget before you write anything, and it removes
the awkwardness from the Closing conversation.

## Guardrails

- **Do not solve the problem here.** The strongest pull in this conversation is to start teaching —
  to explain context engineering, or diagnose why their rollout stalled. That is the product. Name
  the problem precisely; don't hand over the remedy.
- **Do not customize on the fly.** V1 is fixed scope. "Could you also cover X?" → "Let's talk about
  what the standard session covers first."
- **Silence is a tool.** After a question about value, wait. The buyer fills it, usually with the
  most useful sentence of the meeting.
- **Take notes in their language.** Reuse their exact phrases later; it makes the proposal feel
  co-written.

## Exit Criteria

Move to Closing when you can state, in their words, what outcome they want, why it matters, roughly
what it's worth, who decides, and by when. If any of those is missing, stay here.
