## Card 1 — The Wizard & the Labyrinthine Castle

> **Card:** A wizard wants to find the path home and their only lead is a labyrinthine castle, but it will mean shattering an old truce.

### Idea 1 — *The Note Outside the Gate* (Dark Fantasy)

The player finds the staff first. It's leaning against the iron gate with the casual arrangement of something set down for a moment — not abandoned, never abandoned — just resting while its owner steps inside.

The note is tucked beneath it, folded twice in the methodical way of someone who knew they might not be back to explain in person.

*I have been lost forty-three years. I know what the truce cost to make, and I know what breaking it will cost others. I have made my peace with that arithmetic. If you're reading this, it means I went anyway. I'm sorry for the war. I'm not sorry for the going.*

*— Maren, of the Western Fold, if that's even still a place.*

The gate stands open. The castle beyond it is impossibly large and impossibly quiet, the kind of quiet that has been maintained with great effort for a very long time. The old truce-glow — that amber ward the elders described as a permanent sunrise at the threshold — is gone. Where it was, there is only cold air and the faint smell of something ancient beginning to move.

The player steps forward. Then stops.

Somewhere inside, a light moves. Just once — a warm traveling light, like a lantern swinging, going deeper. Not frantic. Purposeful.

She's walking. She knows where she's going.

The castle closes behind her with a sound like a held breath finally released, and then the gate clangs shut with a finality that has nothing to do with locks. The truce-stones along the border wall, every one of them, go dark in sequence, left to right, like a row of candles being pinched out by invisible fingers — all the way to the horizon, all the way to the edge of the long peace that Maren's people died to build and that Maren has now, in forty-three years of quiet desperation, finally spent.

The player carries her staff out. It's a good staff. It hums faintly when pointed east, insistently when pointed west.

There is no east for Maren anymore. There is only the castle, and somewhere inside it, a door that only opens once.

The war begins in three days. Everyone will remember the war. Almost no one will remember why.

### Idea 2 — *The Cardamom Smoke* (Cozy Fantasy)

The first time the player met Aldric, he was at a crossroads inn, flour on his sleeve, feeding the innkeeper's dog bits of a scone he'd made himself that morning.

"I'm looking for a labyrinthine castle," he said, when asked what brought him to the road. "I've been looking for it most of my life." He said it the way someone might say *I've been meaning to organize my bookshelf* — warm, a little rueful, entirely without drama.

He gave the player a hand-drawn map of the next valley (accurate, illustrated with small pictures of notable rocks) and a wax paper parcel of those scones. Cardamom and clove. The kind of recipe a grandmother makes, or a grandmother once made, before she was gone and the kitchen went quiet.

The player passed back through that inn four months later.

"The old wizard found it," the innkeeper said, polishing a glass. "Knocked on the door, they opened it right up. Walked in smiling. Nice to see someone get what they're looking for."

"Has he come back out?"

A pause. "Not yet. But there's smoke from the chimney." She set the glass down. "Smells like baking. We all noticed. Every morning, just at dawn." She frowned at her own hands. "It's a comfort, really. Knowing he's settled in."

The player looked out the window at the distant tower, just visible over the treeline.

The smoke rose thin and pale and sweet. Cardamom. Clove. Exactly that recipe, exactly that smell, gentle and nourishing and endlessly patient.

The innkeeper's dog had stopped eating last week. It just sat by the road with its head tilted, whining softly toward the east.

The player asked how long it had been since anyone had seen Aldric at a window, in the courtyard, on the road.

"Oh, he's in there," the innkeeper said, firmly, in the voice of someone who has decided something and intends to keep deciding it. "He's fine. He's just settled. Some people, when they finally get somewhere they belong — they just stay."

She went back to polishing.

The smoke rose.

The dog whined.

And the player, who had eaten those scones and found them very good, thought about the fact that Aldric had never once mentioned what home was *called*, or who was waiting there, or whether anyone was.

### Idea 3 — *Technically Compliant* (Dark Comedy)

The truce was filed in triplicate with the Office of Neutral Territorial Agreements (Regional Chapter, Midlands Annex), signed by seven parties, notarized by a gnome named Bertrand, and had held for two hundred and eleven years without a single violation, largely because everyone involved was either dead or too tired to care about the castle anymore.

Vorantha was neither dead nor tired. She was methodical, which is worse.

She spent six months in the Annex reading the original document. She found, on page forty-seven, subsection nine, paragraph (c), the following clause: *No living person bearing the soul-signature of a signatory bloodline may pass through the castle's threshold under arms, under enchantment, or under the authority of any recognized magical body.*

She was not under arms. She was not under enchantment.

She resigned from the Thaumaturges' Guild on a Tuesday.

Technically, she was no longer affiliated with any recognized magical body.

Bertrand, who was still alive (gnomes), and who was pulled from retirement to review her appeal, stared at the paperwork for a very long time.

"This is," he said carefully, "technically compliant."

"I know," said Vorantha.

"You understand that the spirit of the truce —"

"I have read the truce. The spirit of the truce is not a legally binding document."

The castle, when she knocked, opened its door with what could only be described as reluctant professional courtesy.

She walked through in forty minutes. The other side: her homeland, more or less. Somewhat changed. Her house was a post office now. Her sister's children were married with children of their own. Her cat had died, been replaced, that replacement had also died, and the neighbors were on their third attempt at a replacement and keeping expectations low.

She stood in the post office for a while.

She bought a stamp.

She walked back to the castle. It opened again, still reluctant, still professional.

The labyrinth, on the return trip, was exactly the same number of steps. She counted.

She found a room near the center that caught good morning light. The castle had one — every castle does, if you look. She set her bag down. She made tea. Outside the window, not quite east and not quite west, the world continued in both directions simultaneously, which she found, after sixty years on the road, to be a reasonable arrangement.

She never tried to leave again.

Bertrand, back in the Annex, filed the paperwork under *Resolved — Unusual*. He made a note in the margin: *See also: the question of what home is for, if not the finding.*

Then he went home himself. It was a Thursday. His wife had made soup. It was very good.

## Card 2 — The True-Hearted Warrior & the Healing Tree

> **Card:** A true-hearted warrior wants to be healed by a tree, but it will mean giving up an important heirloom.

### Idea 1 — *What a Seamstress Knows* (Cozy Fantasy)

The hawthorn is old enough that the road bent around it rather than the other way. You find the old warrior sitting against its roots, eating an apple, looking like a woman who has recently put down something very heavy and is still noticing the absence.

She is old in the way that means *survived everything*. Her mail is dented in three places. The scar along her jaw curves like a question mark.

"My side," she says, before you can ask. "Twelve years I carried a bolt wound from the Siege of Auldmere. The kind that doesn't kill you, just reminds you every morning that it can." She gestures at her ribs. "Gone now."

You ask about the tree.

"The tree asked for my mother's thimble." She says it simply, the way you say things you've thought about long enough that they make sense now. "Little silver thing. She was a seamstress — never held a sword in her life. The thimble was the only thing of hers I kept. Everything else burned or got lost the way things do, in a long life."

You ask if it was hard to give up.

She considers this seriously, which you appreciate.

"Trees don't need thimbles," she says. "But you have to give something you'd choose to die holding, or you're not really asking to live. You're asking to stop hurting. Those are different requests."

She turns the apple core in her fingers.

"My mother used to sew field wounds shut. Right in the camp, calm as mending a shirt. She said a wound is just a seam that opened wrong." A pause. "I think she would have understood."

She doesn't ask you to sit. She doesn't ask you to leave.

In the bark above you, catching the light, there is a small silver shape you almost miss.

Almost.

### Idea 2 — *The Shield Remembers* (Mythic Fantasy)

The World-Root doesn't look like the songs describe. Songs give it light, thunder, a voice that names the worthy. What you find is a tree the size of a cathedral and the silence of a held breath, and a man twenty feet from it who has already made his peace and is just waiting for his legs to finish the conversation.

His hands are stone. Both of them, wrist to fingertip — grey granite veined with quartz, curled into a resting position that only looks like gauntlets until it doesn't.

"Spread from the hands," he says, without turning. "Three years ago. A dying war-god got the last word before I took his head. Every battle since, a little more stone." He turns, and his face is just a face — tired, bearded, someone's grandfather. "The World-Root stopped it."

You look at the bark. Something is embedded there: a shield, bronze-rimmed, battered, ancient.

"My grandfather made it," he says. "Carried it at the Third Crossing. After him, my father. After him, me. It never broke." A pause. "The tree didn't *take* it, exactly. It showed me the shield wasn't keeping the curse out. It was keeping it *in*. Three years of thinking I was fighting. Turns out I was just — containing."

You ask about the stone hands.

He holds them up. In their way, they are extraordinary — every tendon rendered in rock, every knuckle mapped in quartz.

"The tree healed everything the curse had taken that I wanted back," he says.

Just that.

You look at the shield in the bark. You look at his hands. You try to read something in his face that tells you whether he found peace or simply ran out of the energy required to call it anything else.

He walks south. His stone fingers leave no prints in the mud.

### Idea 3 — *Her Name Was Sefa* (Gothic)

The Silver Oak has stood here longer than the city that burned around it. You can see the ash-line on the bark three feet up, from the Culling. The tree survived. The city did not.

She has been standing twelve paces from the trunk for four days. You know this from the merchants who set up camp nearby, watched, and finally left.

You might mistake her for a statue if statues bled. The plague brightens the veins — works its way up through the capillaries like ink pressed into wet paper. She is very far along. Her neck is a map of dark rivers. Her eyes, when they find yours, are still clear.

In her right hand she holds a locket on a chain. It hangs open. The portrait inside is too small to see from here.

"Don't," she says. Her voice is very steady. "I know what everyone says."

You don't say anything.

"My daughter was seven." Not to you — to the tree, or to the locket, or to no one. "The plague came through her village in one night. By the time I rode—" The locket hand tightens. "I've been a warrior for twenty-two years. I've given the Order my youth. I gave the Hold my health. I gave the war my husband. Those felt like choices. This doesn't feel like a choice."

The Silver Oak waits. It is very good at waiting.

"If I give it the portrait, there will be nothing left of her in this world. No marked grave. No one who remembers her name but me, and the plague is already—" She touches her temple. "Here."

The darkness in her veins is moving. You can see it now.

She holds the locket up.

"Her name was Sefa," she tells the portrait. "You had your father's nose. You laughed at things that weren't funny, and you were always right that they were."

Her hand moves — slowly, slowly — toward the bark.

You realize you don't know whether you hope she does it or doesn't.

You realize that might be the crueler part.

The roots shift.

The locket swings.

## Card 3 — The Burdened Behemoth & the Sanctuary

> **Card:** A burdened behemoth wants to pledge their life in service to a sanctuary, but they will suffer an injury that does not heal.

### Idea 1 — *What the Pool Remembers* (Folklore)

There is a shrine in the oldest part of the forest where the trees are so tall that daylight arrives an hour after everyone else's. You find it because the path turns toward it whether you mean it to or not.

At the edge of the reflecting pool kneels something enormous. It is roughly the shape of a bear and roughly the shape of a man and entirely neither, and it has been kneeling long enough that mossy roots have threaded through its knees and anchored it to the earth. Its left side, from shoulder to hip, is open — not freshly, not ragged, but in the way of old wounds that have become part of the landscape, like the crevasse in a cliff face. Something clear runs from it into the pool and spreads slowly.

It does not look up when you approach. It says, without preamble: "I have waited longer than you have been."

It came here after the last war. It says *the* last war with the certainty of something that has watched several candidates for that title and formed opinions. It came to give its life to the shrine. It knelt. The wound opened. It has not been able to stand since.

"Does the shrine know you're here?" you ask.

A long silence. The pool does not reflect properly. The depths show something you can't quite see.

"The shrine knew before I did," it says.

When you leave, it is still kneeling. You do not know if you have spoken to a guardian or a prisoner — and you notice, only after the trees swallow the shrine behind you, that you never found out its name.

The pool doesn't say. The pool only reflects.

### Idea 2 — *The Right Direction* (Literary)

The healing house smells of lye soap, boiled linen, and three years of campfrey poultice. Brother Aldric — the monks gave him that name and he kept it because his old name had been part of something else — is sixty-two and built like a granary: solid, load-bearing, patient in the way that things are patient when they've seen too much to be surprised.

He moves slowly between the cots. The people in those cots don't flinch from him, which took them time but which they managed. His right arm does not straighten above the elbow, a wound from a winter campaign in a valley he does not name. It was set badly. He had still been fighting then.

The youngest novice — she is perhaps fifteen and has opinions about everything — asks him why he stays.

He wrings out a cloth. He does not look annoyed by the question. He has been asked it before and expects to be asked it again.

"I spent a long time making wounds," he says. "It seemed like the right direction to travel."

She waits, the way the young do when they sense there is more.

"It isn't repentance," he says, which she didn't ask. "It isn't punishment either. I just found that this was the thing I could still do. And the quiet here is a different kind."

She asks what kind.

"The first morning I woke up," he says, "and the first thing I heard wasn't screaming." He folds the cloth. "That's the kind."

He carries the basin to the next cot. She sits for a while with his answer, turning it over. It has no rough edges. She cannot find a way in that hasn't already been accounted for.

### Idea 3 — *Nothing Wrong With the Light* (Cosmic Horror)

The temple should not be here, geometrically speaking, but here it is. The spire is stone. The courtyard is stone. The gate is stone and also not stone, in a way you register but cannot explain, like a word you understood until you tried to define it.

At the gate sits a creature the size of a small tower, folded into a configuration that suggests rest. You count its limbs twice and get different answers. These things you notice but they do not frighten you, which will bother you later. What you notice most, and first, and keep noticing, is its face — which is a face, recognizably, and which is patient. Old in the way that patience is old when it has stopped being a virtue and has become a geology.

Its wound is in the center of its chest, wide as a door, and from it falls light that is not quite light — upward, slowly, into nothing.

"Welcome," it says. Its voice is not frightening at all. That is the thing. "This place is for those who need rest."

You ask about the wound. It looks down at its chest with the mild curiosity of someone who has found a button missing from a familiar coat.

"It opened when I pledged," it says. "It does not close."

"Does it hurt?"

It considers. "There is nothing wrong with the light here," it says, in the tone of an answer.

You don't know what that means. This seems fine to it.

You leave. The journey back takes a normal amount of time. You are fine.

Except you find yourself, weeks later, looking for the light. Not that light — the real word for it hasn't arrived yet. You look in lamplight, in the pale stripe under a door before dawn, in the way certain late afternoons go gold and strange. You know you're looking when you catch yourself slowing down, leaning toward some ordinary radiance, certain for just a moment that this is it —

It never is.

You don't stop looking.

## Card 4 — The Poisoned Archer & the Ship

> **Card:** A poisoned archer wants to stop an enemy from reaching a ship, but it will mean laying down arms forever.

### Idea 1 — *What the Tide Brought In* (Atmospheric)

Maren is sitting at the end of Pier Six when you find her, bow flat across her thighs, quiver empty in the puddle beside her. She doesn't turn when you approach. The morning fog is still thick off the water. Half a mile out, a warship sits at anchor — not moored, not moving, not going anywhere.

"General's on the dock," she says. "Bandaged. Screaming, probably, though I can't hear him anymore."

She pauses.

"He'll live. That was always the idea."

The poison in her is three weeks old. The apothecary had given her a year, then revised it to months, then stopped revising. Taking the shot had cost her the use of her arms — not metaphorically. The muscle-curse bound to the arrowhead had drawn from her when she loosed it. She had known it would. She had made the trade before she ever nocked the arrow.

The shot had landed just before dawn. Ninety yards, in the dark, with hands that had been trembling for three days. The general went down. The sailors panicked. The ship pulled anchor without him. It will not return before the council messengers arrive.

You can ask what she was stopping him from.

"War," she says. "A big one. The kind that chews through people like her and spits out the ones who started it." She sounds tired — not bitter. Tired the way someone gets when they have finally reached the place they were going. "Council had the votes to negotiate. They just needed three more days. Now they have them."

She asks you to help her stand. She does not ask you to carry the bow.

You leave it on the dock. The tide comes in slow. By the time you reach the top of the pier steps, the water is over the planks, and the bow is floating — not sinking, just drifting — pointing out to sea, toward where the ship should have gone.

Maren doesn't look back.

### Idea 2 — *The Name in the Piling* (Mythic Fantasy)

There is a story told in the port towns about an archer who sold her name to the War-Remembrancer in exchange for arrows that never missed. The price was this: the last arrow she ever fired would take everything with it — her name, her face in the memories of those who had known her, the notch in her bow where forty years of thumb had worn the wood smooth.

You find the fire first. Small, built in the lee of a chandler's wall, still warm. Coals, not ash. Someone was here within the hour.

Near the gangplank of Pier Four, there is an arrowhead embedded in the wood. Just the head. The shaft and the feathers are gone — not broken away, gone, as if they were never there. The wound in the plank is clean, like old damage, except it still smells of pitch-smoke and something you cannot name.

On the piling, four letters are cut with a belt knife — deep, deliberate, the kind of mark someone makes when they are thinking carefully about being forgotten. A name. Except no one in the port recognizes it when asked. The dockmaster checks the ledgers. The night watch doesn't remember anyone on the pier before dawn.

The ship that was berthed here — the one the general was meant to board — is visible in the outer roads. Turned about. Sailing back the way it came. What turned it, the harbormaster will not say, or cannot. Both look the same from the outside.

There is no body. There is no bow. There is a fire going cold and a name in a post that no one seems to have cut, and an arrowhead in wood that looks the way old things look when time has had its way with them.

You can take the name with you. It is the only thing left of her.

Whether that was enough — whether she thought it would be, in the end — is not something the harbor will tell you.

### Idea 3 — *One More Notch* (Noir)

The rain makes it hard to hear, and Dasha is glad for that.

She is pressed into the gap between the chandler's warehouse and the rope-store, in the dark, one knee in a puddle that stopped being cold an hour ago because she stopped being able to feel her legs. The bow is up. The arrow is nocked. She has eleven arrows left in the quiver, but there will only be one shot worth taking tonight.

She hears you before she sees you — your footsteps telegraphing on the wet boards — and she says, quietly, without turning: "If you're going to stop me, do it in the next minute or don't bother."

She has been in the port three days, waiting. The poison started at the Battle of Ember Fork, worked into her by a field-surgeon's arrow meant for someone else. The healers are very clear: she has weeks. The shot will take what the poison hasn't yet — the last of her muscle memory, her hands, the ability to draw again, ever. Her body knows this. It doesn't want to cooperate.

She tells you who the target is. A man who coordinated a burning upriver. A village. She says the name of it once, quietly.

"Someone should remember it," she says.

You can tell her it's not her job. That the council has him on a list. That there are proper channels.

She doesn't answer that.

A figure appears at the far end of the pier. Unhurried. Moving toward the gangplank of a fat trader's vessel. He stops to adjust his cloak against the rain — standing still, hood thrown back.

Dasha takes a long, slow breath. You watch her hands. They are shaking, and then, for just a moment, they are not.

She raises the bow.

## Card 5 — The Bandit & the Expansive Empire

> **Card:** A bandit wants to sabotage an enemy with an expansive empire, but they will burn out the last of their power.

### Idea 1 — *The Weight of the Last Working* (Epic Fantasy)

The player finds Sorrel Vane sitting against the support post of a collapsed grain tower, eating a heel of bread like someone with nowhere to be and no reason to hurry.

The tower is still smoking. The imperial grain stores that fed three legions — gone. The manifest records burned with them. By the time the empire retallies what it lost, the winter will have done the rest.

"You did this," the player might say.

Sorrel Vane shrugs. "Braid used to do it for me. She and I, we had an arrangement." She taps the hollow of her collarbone, where a faint scar runs — old, deliberate, like a signature. "She's gone now. Used her all up."

Braid was what she called it. A second presence she'd carried since she was fourteen, half-curse and half-gift, the reason her fires never missed and her escapes always had a way through. She'd spent forty years rationing it, stealing only what mattered, leaving the small jobs to cleverness and luck she'd made herself.

"Could have kept her," Sorrel says. "Could've. But there's a pass in the Seldrath Range — one of the good ones, not on imperial maps yet. There's a village there that doesn't exist on paper. There's a reason it doesn't." She looks at her hands. "They needed this winter to be a cold one for the legions. So."

She's not asking for thanks. She isn't performing anything. The bread is the whole gesture, and the rest is just aftermath.

You can offer her coin. She'll take it. You can ask what she plans to do now. "Haven't needed to plan in a long time," she says. "Might be interesting."

She doesn't look afraid. That's the thing you'll remember later, when the empire is still standing, is still building roads through the Seldrath, is still finding villages that never should have been findable. You'll remember that she wasn't afraid, that the bread was good, and that she ate it like someone who had finished something.

Some things end quietly. Some things finish you and you eat bread in the snow and that's the shape of it.

### Idea 2 — *Meridian* (Gothic)

The crossroads inn has only one guest now: a person who will not give a name because they can no longer remember which one they were using.

They have documents spread across every table in the common room — imperial survey maps, road commission ledgers, chain measurements by the thousand — and all of them are wrong. The player will notice this if they look. The distances don't match. The waypoints contradict each other. A road that should curve south curves north instead, then loops back to itself in a way that suggests the cartographer was drunk, or cursed, or both.

"I did that," the traveler says, not looking up. Their ink-stained fingers trace a route that leads nowhere real. "Every copy. Every backup survey. Every seal in every regional office. I put my hand on the maps and I put in errors only a surveyor would know to look for, not know to correct."

They were called Duskline. Or Moth. One of those names that sticks in the low places — thief markets, bridge camps, the kind of roads that don't appear on official surveys. They had a gift: a perfect internal sense of place, of orientation, of distance traveled. They always knew where they were. More than that — they always knew where they weren't. In the empire's records, they could feel the wrongness of official cartography like a splinter in a tooth.

"I burned it all correcting the errors in the other direction," they say. "Fed it backward through the gift and out." They press both palms flat to the table. "Now I don't know where the door is."

The door is behind them and to the left. The player can see it plainly. Whether to say so is their own business.

Outside, somewhere, imperial supply columns are following roads that spiral inward. Whether the delays become disasters or are simply rerouted by local commanders smart enough to mistrust the maps — no one in this inn can say.

Duskline is looking at the documents like they're trying to remember a word in a language they no longer speak. They might be here tomorrow. They might walk out the door that isn't behind them and simply not come back.

The maps are wrong. The roads are wrong.

What that buys anyone is a question the gift can no longer answer.

### Idea 3 — *Nine Thousand Forms in Triplicate* (Social Satire)

The player meets Ven Carrick in the back of a notary office, surrounded by official documentation, looking like someone who has just finished something they cannot explain to anyone at a party.

"You're a bandit?" they say, because the player, presumably, has heard this.

Ven Carrick gestures at the walls. "I was a number-reader," they say. "I could look at an imperial census and feel what was false in it. Not a metaphor. I mean I could taste the wrong digit from across a room. The empire runs on counting. On knowing exactly how many heads in how many villages. Find a village; tax the village; conscript the village; bleed the village dry on schedule." They tap a stack of ledgers. "I spent fourteen years learning which offices talk to which other offices. Which regional administrator is afraid of which imperial auditor. Which census entries can be wrong in ways that cascade upward."

"You falsified records," the player might say.

"I falsified records," Ven Carrick agrees, pleasantly. "Fourteen years. And then last month I poured everything I had left — every bit of the gift — into making the falsifications unfindable. Permanent. Self-reinforcing." They pause. "The village of Thresk Hollow does not exist, according to the empire. It did not exist last year. It has never existed. There are forty-three people in Thresk Hollow."

The gift is gone now. Ven Carrick cannot read numbers anymore. They tried this morning with the notary ledger and felt nothing. Just marks on a page.

"Does it matter?" the player might ask. Maybe they mean the village. Maybe they mean the gift.

"Thresk Hollow matters," Ven Carrick says. Then, after a pause: "The empire has seventeen hundred census districts. I protected one village. In one district. From one cycle of conscription." They fold their hands. "So. No. Systemically. No."

They don't look devastated by this. They look like someone who has done a very specific thing very well and understood that it was very small, and done it anyway, and would do it again, and cannot, and that's the whole of it.

The empire's next census begins in spring. Thresk Hollow is safe for now. What "now" means when you're forty-three people against a seventeen-hundred-district apparatus is a problem for a different conversation.

Ven Carrick goes back to the forms. They can't feel numbers anymore but they can still count. It's slower. It'll do.

## Card 6 — The Invoker & the Radiant Aerie

> **Card:** An invoker wants to map (or map the history of) a radiant aerie, but one way or another, it will be their last adventure.

### Idea 1 — *The Weight of a Finished Map* (Literary)

Seven trips. Forty years. Maren Ashpike had mapped the Radiant Aerie in layers, the way you read a person — first the shape of them, then the patterns, then finally the thing underneath that the patterns were protecting.

She was in the Verdant Archive when the player found her. Not hiding. Just there. A cup of tea gone cold, a ring of parchment rolls spread across the table like a clock face. She was annotating margins in a hand so small you had to lean in to read it.

"You're here for the maps," she said, without looking up. It wasn't a question.

She slid the final chart across the table. It was beautiful in the way genuinely useful things sometimes are — every elevation precisely marked, every lightwell notated, the spiral corridors labeled in her personal shorthand with translations in the margins. She had mapped the aerie's seasons across four decades. The document knew the place the way the place knew itself.

"The last time I went up," she said, "I knew it was the last time. Not because I was afraid. Not because anything happened. Because I finally had enough."

The player asked if she was ever afraid.

She thought about it. "The third trip. The light came through a crack in the eastern face and I couldn't move for an hour. But I kept my pen moving." She tapped the chart. "Most of this is in those margins."

She asked what the player intended to do with the maps. She asked the way a person asks who has already accepted the answer, whatever it might be.

When they left, she had turned back to her tea and her annotations. She was not sad. She was not celebrating. She was a person who had finished something large and was sitting inside the strange quiet of completion — the way you sit in a room after a long gathering, when the guests have left and the candles are nearly out, and there's no reason yet to rise.

### Idea 2 — *What the Aerie Wrote* (Cosmic Horror)

Ssel Vorthane's study looked, at first, like the study of a careful man. The maps were rolled and labeled. The ledgers were numbered by date. The ink was consistent — the same blue-black he'd used since his apprenticeship, a color you couldn't find anymore.

Except.

The maps from his early surveys were beautiful but ordinary: elevation, light-angle, the notations of an invoker cataloguing spirit-corridors and resonance points. But the maps from his last three visits had something else in them. The handwriting had begun to change. Not in a way you'd notice immediately — just a slight elongation of certain letters, a pressure difference in the downstrokes.

By the final sheet, it was not his handwriting at all.

The final map was perfect. The player could tell without being able to say how — the proportions were exact in a way that went beyond technique. There were annotations in a language that predated every archive the player knew. And in the bottom margin, in something that wanted to be Vorthane's hand: *I understand now what I was measuring.*

Vorthane was in the reading chair by the window. Alive. Breathing slowly and with tremendous regularity, the way you breathe in deep sleep. His eyes were open. His hands were folded in his lap. When the player touched his arm, he was warm — very warm, the temperature of sunlit stone.

In the dark of the study, barely perceptible, the skin along his forearms glowed.

The player could take the maps. They were complete. They were accurate. Whoever had finished them — whoever had leaned over this desk in Vorthane's body and understood the aerie's intentions and transcribed them in a dead language — had done excellent work.

Vorthane's chest rose. Vorthane's chest fell.

The light in his arms was the same color as the aerie's corridors, if the player had ever seen them.

The player might have seen them.

### Idea 3 — *She Is Not Done* (Gothic)

The physician had given Daelith Farrow eight weeks. She had taken three of them to climb the Radiant Aerie and complete her final survey. She told no one it would be her last. Everyone who knew her knew anyway.

When she hadn't come down after two weeks, her apprentice hired the player.

The aerie was worse in approach than in arrival. From below it was all brightness and impossible geometry, light bleeding off its edges in bands that left afterimages on the inside of the eye. Up close, it was quieter. The light was a color without a name. The path was clear, which was strange. Aeries rarely made themselves easy.

The player found her at the summit, in the center of the great radiance-ring, sitting with her back straight and her maps spread around her in a wide circle, every edge precisely weighted with invocation anchors. The spirits she had summoned were still active — pale geometries of light that moved along invisible tracks and reported back at intervals. She was working.

She looked up when the player arrived. She looked — the player had to stop and consider the word — *better.*

"I need another week," she said.

They told her about the physician's estimate. She nodded as if she already knew.

"Look at my hands," she said.

The tremor the patient files mentioned was gone. The bluish discoloration, documented in the last examination, had faded to nothing. Her hands moved across the parchment with the precision of her prime, thirty years back.

"Whatever is in the light here—" She paused. "I'm not claiming it's permanent. I'm claiming I'm not finished."

The player looked at the maps. They were extraordinary. They were also perhaps a week from completion — from the kind of completion that meant something, that a library would keep, that would outlast everything.

The light moved quietly in her hands. Her pen moved. The spirits made their silent circuits.

The player did not know how to take her home.

## Card 7 — The Swashbuckler & the Draconian Cavern

> **Card:** A swashbuckler wants to claim the hidden artifact of a draconian cavern, but they must make a sacrifice no one will ever know about.

### Idea 1 — *No Applause in the Deep* (Dark Fantasy)

You find him outside the cavern mouth, sitting on a frost-cracked boulder with his boots off and his rapier across his knees. The artifact — a scale-shaped reliquary, draconic silver, humming faintly with whatever it contains — sits beside him wrapped in the lining of his cloak.

He hears you approach and stands. The rapier vanishes into its scabbard with a showman's flourish. He opens his mouth.

Nothing comes out.

He closes it. Tries again. Silence — not hoarse silence, not laryngitis silence, but the deep acoustic *absence* of a voice that has been taken, not lost. Like a room where there used to be music.

He watches your face process this. Then he does something unexpected: he laughs. Or performs the shape of laughing — the shoulders, the thrown-back head, the whole physical argument for joy. No sound. He reaches into his doublet and produces a small notebook, flips to a prepared page, and holds it up.

*"Worth it,"* it says. *"Don't ask how much."*

You look at the reliquary. You look at him. There's a wall of dragon-claw runes visible just inside the cavern entrance — old draconic contract-law, the kind that demanded an offering commensurate with what was taken. He'd walked in with the finest instrument he owned: a voice that could quiet a taproom or rouse a regiment, that could sell a lie in four languages and make a king weep with the truth. He'd given it to the stone and the old draconian silence.

No one will ever know. You know, now, but there's no tale he can tell to make it his. He can't describe the vault, can't recount the moment, can't do the voice. The story is locked inside a man who can no longer speak it.

He holds out the reliquary. His grin is still there. It is possibly the most heroic expression you have ever seen on a human face, and it is absolutely killing him.

You take the artifact.

He bows — deep, theatrical, performance-perfect — to an audience of rocks and you, and you get the sense he is practicing for a life of precisely this: grand gestures witnessed only by the indifferent and the obligated.

You leave. Behind you, you can almost hear him not saying anything at all.

### Idea 2 — *The Many Names of Nobody Famous* (Noir)

The barkeep at the Struck Bell says he was called Aldric Voss. Paid in silver, talked too much, left a knife in the wall as a calling card. Very charming. Very irritating. Fixed the draconian problem.

The ferryman two miles east says his name was Cobb Fairweather. Similar description: silver buckles, dueling fashion, never stopped talking. Gave him a gold coin and said something like *"tell them Cobb Fairweather was here"* — which the ferryman found odd, since there was no one to tell.

The shrine-keeper near the cavern trail says he announced himself as Marchetti — just Marchetti — and asked if the shrine had any dragon-law texts. When she produced one, he read it, nodded, and said, *"Mm. That's what I thought."* He did not seem troubled. He seemed, she said, like a man settling an account he'd known about for years.

The draconian vault's lock required a true name. Not a password — a *name*. The oldest contract-law: a name is a tether, a weight, a thing that can be given. The vault's asking price was the name of whoever opened it. Not their life. Not their blood. Just the self-label, the *I am* that organizes a person's past and future into something navigable.

He gave it. Walked in under one name. Walked out under none.

You find him at a crossroads inn three days later. He's sitting alone, which is wrong for someone dressed like that. You approach. He looks up, and the charm assembles itself automatically — the smile, the posture, the slight tilt of the head that says *I am about to be interesting at you.*

"What's your name?" you ask.

He pauses. Genuinely pauses. "I have several," he says, pleasantly. "What sort of name were you hoping for?"

He buys you a drink. He tells you an excellent story about someone else entirely. You never find out if he's lost or free, and you're not certain he knows the difference anymore — or that he'd tell you, even if he did.

### Idea 3 — *Everything Except the Reason* (Psychological Thriller)

The draconian vault is empty now. Clean. The bones of whatever used to live here have been arranged with some care — laid out along the walls with a reverence that wasn't in the old reports. Someone tidied the dead.

She's sitting in the center of the chamber on an overturned shield, the reliquary balanced on her knee, turning it over and over with the practiced fingers of someone very comfortable with fine things. She is dressed for a performance: silver-buckled, expensively scarred, manifestly equipped for daring. She looks up when you enter and her expression opens into something warm and professional.

"Hello," she says. "Is this yours?"

She holds out the reliquary. You take it, because you need it and because nothing about the way she's offering it suggests she thinks it should be otherwise.

"Did you retrieve this?" you ask.

"I think so," she says. "I know how to open things like this. I appear to have done so." She looks around the chamber with mild, friendly puzzlement. "I'm not entirely certain why I came here. I have a feeling there was a reason. A good one." She smiles. "I'm sure it'll come back to me."

She's been in the cavern for what looks like three days based on the supplies stacked near the far wall. She doesn't remember the outside. She doesn't remember the name of whoever hired her. She has a letter in her breast pocket — you can see the edge — and when she reaches for it, her hand stops.

"That's private," she says. She doesn't open it. She doesn't know why it's private. She just knows.

The cavern took her purpose and left everything else: the skills, the charm, the three days of tidy, private grief she has apparently performed on dragon bones. She is a performance with no story to be in.

You have the artifact. You have no idea who sent her. The reliquary hums in your hands like something unfinished.

Behind you, she asks, pleasantly:

"Do you know how to get out of here?"

## Card 8 — The Rogue & the Merfolk Market

> **Card:** A rogue wants to prevent a war from being started by a merfolk market, but they will have to act against their religion.

### Idea 1 — *The Stone Above the Tide-Mark* (Mythic Fantasy)

The Saltsworn hold twelve commandments, but three stand above the others like pillars holding up the vault of faith: do not steal from the Deepspeaker market; do not speak falsehood in the merfolk tongue; do not lay hands upon their sacred vessels. The merfolk are the Voice of Aethon-beneath-the-water. To violate their commerce is to violate the god himself. Every child in a salt chapel learns this before they learn to swim.

Tessara Venn kept all three for twenty-six years.

She broke them in just over twenty minutes.

You find her prayer stone above the high-tide mark on a flat shelf of rock east of Vel Shan's harbor — wedged into a crack with deliberate precision, positioned so the sea cannot reach it even at storm-flood. The stone is black basalt, smooth from decades of daily handling, the kind Saltsworn pilgrims carry from their first oath-day to their funeral. It bears the tide-wheel of Aethon on its face. On the underside, in the fine cuts of a work knife: *Aethon — I know what I did. I would do it again. I do not yet know what that means about either of us. Wait for me.*

The harbormaster calls it providence. The war-tide draught — a vial that, slipped into the Lantherni water supply, would have dissolved the memory of every treaty their commanders had sworn — shattered on the Deepspeaker's trade-floor when no one was watching. The sale never closed. The Lantherni war council, who needed that vial to keep their generals committed, dissolved in confusion. The ships turned around.

No one in the market saw a thief. They saw, some said, a trick of light between moments.

Tessara was found three days east in a fishing village, arranging passage to the Salt Temple of her childhood order. She went willingly when the adjudicants arrived. She had expected them. She carried no possessions except her robe and an empty space at her belt where a prayer stone had been.

Her confessor recorded her first words: *"I am asking my god a question. I intend to keep asking until I receive an answer, or I die, or I conclude that the silence itself is an answer."*

The stone is still there. The tide rises toward it every twelve hours. It hasn't reached it yet.

### Idea 2 — *The Ledger of Old Names* (Gothic)

The Ash-Walker creed is grief made doctrine. One of the Fourteen Sorrows — the sixth — concerns the dead: *I will not traffic in names that can no longer answer.* The names of the deceased are sacred weight. To speak them in commerce, to buy or sell the right to invoke them, is to bind their souls permanently to the transaction. The dead become a thing valued and priced. The creed calls it a second death.

Maren Coldtallow carries the sixth sorrow along her left forearm in iron-black ink. She was seventeen when she took the oaths. She is thirty-one now, and she spent this past Spring Tide night in the Tidemarket of Vel Shan purchasing the name-rights to forty-three dead villagers from a merfolk war-caller named Ssiveth-Who-Waits.

You find her at the Ebb-and-Rest tavern six hours later. The ledger of sale sits open in her lap. She keeps touching it with two fingers, lightly, the way a person touches a wound that might be serious but isn't bleeding yet.

The names belonged to the people of Harthill, massacred by Lantherni soldiers three years ago. Ssiveth-Who-Waits had acquired them on a blood-debt and intended to sell them to a surface warlord who planned to use a war-caller's invocation to raise the dead as tide-soldiers and march them into the coastal cities that once belonged to them. The war they would start would not be theirs. They would not choose it. They would simply be made to walk.

Maren bought the names. All forty-three. She spoke each one aloud in the merfolk tongue to confirm the sale. She felt each one leave her mouth like a stone dropped into water.

She doesn't know if she saved them. The names are hers now, inked in the ledger, purchased in commerce, exactly as the sixth sorrow forbids. She doesn't know if they're more trapped than before or finally protected. Ssiveth-Who-Waits is gone into the deep market. The warlord's agents left empty-handed.

She is looking at the water through the tavern window. She hasn't ordered a drink.

When you ask if it worked, she considers you for a long moment before answering.

"I know what I stopped," she says. "I don't know what I did."

She looks back at the window.

### Idea 3 — *Tell Me There's Another Way* (Noir)

The room smells like salt and expensive lamp oil and the kind of money that doesn't need to announce itself. The merfolk trade-agent — a half-tide broker named Iseth, legally a citizen of the coastal territory, technically a neutral party — is asleep on the bed. She arrived at the Inn of the Weighted Anchor three hours ago after a very long and successful season, and she will not wake up for six more hours unless something stops that.

The deal she signed today will start a war by morning tide. Not because she's a bad person — she may be a fine person, for all anyone knows — but because the blood-rights contract she brokered gives the Lantherni naval guild access to the deepsea channels that belong to the Southern Tideborn clans by ancient pact. By morning, when the tide-call goes out, those channels become contested. By afternoon, the first Tideborn scouts will find the Lantherni corvettes. The scouts will not be allowed to leave.

Carrick Sorn stands beside the bed. He has been standing there for a while.

In his right hand: a knife, plain-handled, without sentiment.

In his left: prayer beads, carved tortoiseshell, worn smooth in the way of an object handled daily for many years. He found faith at twenty-two in a penal colony on the outer islands, in the doctrine of the Unscorched — a pacific creed that requires converts to renounce all taking of life. He renounced it. He meant it. He has not killed in nine years.

There are no other options left. He has tried every other option. That is how he ended up here, in this room, at this hour.

You open the door without thinking — a wrong room, a borrowed key, a moment of ordinary confusion. You see him: knife in one hand, beads clicking slowly in the other, something in his face that is past deliberation and not yet done. Not the settled calm of a man completing a task. Something rawer than that.

He doesn't look surprised to see you. He looks, if anything, relieved.

He says: *"Tell me there's another way."*

He is absolutely sincere. The beads are still moving.

It's your move.

## Card 9 — The Mimic & the Inn

> **Card:** A mimic wants to enchant an inn, but they will bring darkness to those whose paths they cross.

### Idea 1 — *The Quieter Word* (Cozy Fantasy)

Maren still talked about the inn at Wellmere Crossing the way people talked about a particularly good dream — warmly, and with the specific sadness of something you couldn't quite return to.

"The innkeeper knew my mother's name," she said, pouring you a second cup without being asked. Old habit. She'd picked it up somewhere. "I hadn't mentioned her. I hadn't mentioned anything, really. I sat down at the hearth and he looked at me — really looked — and said, *Elise would have loved this fire.* And I just—" She stopped. Smiled at her cup. "I cried. I don't know why. It felt like being found."

She'd stayed four days. The food was always what you wanted before you knew you wanted it. The music in the common room was somehow always the song you'd half-forgotten from childhood. The other guests laughed easily, fell into conversation as though they'd always known each other.

"I've thought about it since," she said. "How he knew. I thought maybe I talked in my sleep. Or maybe there was something in the wine — not unpleasantly, just..." She trailed off.

The thing she didn't say, and that you only noticed because you were watching for it: she'd moved twice during the conversation to predict your needs — to refill your cup before it emptied, to push the bread forward before you reached — and both times she'd caught herself. Like a gesture she'd learned and not quite finished unlearning.

"Are you all right?" you asked.

"Yes," she said. And then: "I've been a little quieter since. Not unhappy. Just — there's a word I used to say all the time. My favorite word, I think. I can't remember it now. I keep reaching for it and it isn't there."

She laughed, embarrassed.

"Grief does strange things," she said, because Elise had been dead four years and it was easier to blame grief than to name something she had no name for.

You didn't tell her what you suspected. What would you say? *The innkeeper stole a piece of you and you'll never know which piece.* Or: *The innkeeper borrowed some warmth from your life to build a better fire, and maybe it grew back and maybe it didn't.*

Some debts aren't knowable. Some gifts aren't free. The inn at Wellmere Crossing was genuinely wonderful. You believe that.

You just don't know if that's enough.

### Idea 2 — *The Names That Come Back* (Gothic)

The ledger was already there when Vess bought the Wayward Hart. The sellers told her to burn it, but they'd said it the way people say things they don't believe.

She put it on the reception desk and thought nothing of it until the third week, when a name appeared overnight that hadn't been there before. She'd had no guest.

The handwriting was different for each entry — not in the way different people would write their own names, but in the way a very good forger might write them, capturing something essential in each hand. The entry for *Bram and Corlin Ashford, room four* had a restless quality, the loops almost but not quite symmetrical. The entry for *Sister Verity of the Third Path, room seven* had the deliberate precision of someone trained to write and very tired of it.

The new name was her late husband's. The date was three years after he had died.

She didn't burn the ledger.

More names came. Never guests she'd had, always in handwriting that fit them perfectly, though she had never met them and did not know their hands. She stopped asking how the ledger knew and started keeping track. The names clustered near times of mist. If she left a candle lit overnight, there were more names by morning.

She told herself the ledger was a record of past guests, wandering guests, guests from before she'd arrived. She told herself there was an explanation she hadn't found yet.

Last Tuesday: her own name. *Vess, room eleven. No preference for pillow.*

This morning: her own name again. Same room. Below it, in her own handwriting — she was certain it was her own handwriting — a note she had not written: *enjoyed the fire, will stay again.*

Room eleven was at the end of the hall, past the storage, in the part of the inn she kept locked. She'd always assumed the habit was practical. She was beginning to think it might have been instinct.

She took the key from the hook.

The door at the end of the hall had light beneath it.

She turned the key. She opened the door.

### Idea 3 — *Everything the Cartographer Learned to Draw* (Magical Realism)

Ysel had not meant to map the road to Dusk Hollow. She'd never been there. She'd been mapping the northern coast, a Merchant's Council commission, head down over her desk in the inn at Throsswater, when she'd looked up and found thirty pages of somewhere else.

Dusk Hollow, as it turned out, was real. She checked the Archives. The town was correctly placed, its features matching hers with a precision no guesser could manage. She had drawn its streets from the inside, the way a person draws a place they love: the fire-stains on the hearthstones, the angle of the inn-sign where the hinge had rusted, the creak-path through the floorboards that avoided waking the couple in room three.

By the time you found her, she'd mapped six towns she'd never visited. All of them had inns. All of them had that same quality — the details of someone who had wanted, badly, to make a home.

"I think it was copying me," she said, without alarm. She'd had three months to reach this conclusion. "My way of paying attention. My eyes." She spread her hands. "I thought it would feel like a violation. It doesn't. It mostly feels like correspondence. Like something very far away is writing me letters by drawing through my hands."

She gave you the most recent map. "I don't need it. I'll draw another one when it moves on. Give it this, if you find it. So it knows the letters arrived."

You asked if she wanted the copying to stop.

She thought about it for a long time.

"The strange thing," she said finally, "is that I'm drawing better now than I did before. Like having another eye opened alongside mine." A pause. "Though I suppose it was always my eye. It just borrowed it for a while."

She went back to her desk. The northern coast survey waited. She picked up her pen and held it over the page, and for a moment her hand didn't move — testing the space between one kind of attention and another, checking whether what remained was still hers.

Then she drew. Slowly. Carefully.

Herself, or close enough.

## Card 10 — The Dicer & the Dwarven Empire

> **Card:** A dicer wants to pay an old debt to/with a dwarven empire, but it will open old wounds.

### Idea 1 — *What the Hollow Left Behind* (Noir)

The barkeep at the Iron Soot has a lip scar and the silence of a man who saw something three nights ago and hasn't decided what to do with it yet.

The table in the corner — the one with the good view of both doors. Gamblers always take that table. On it: a set of dice, bone-white, slightly too heavy when you pick them up. Loaded. The kind you carry for forty years as insurance, and then just leave.

His name was the Hollow. Not a real name — just the shape of a reputation. He came three nights ago to settle a debt with Velchin Brass, third-generation instrument of a judgment handed down by a dead man. Velchin's grandfather lost a game. The debt passed like an inheritance no one wanted.

They played one hand of Clan-Crossing — the old game, the binding one, the kind dwarves use for things that matter. The Hollow lost.

The barkeep thinks it was deliberate. Maybe it wasn't — maybe forty years of carrying a weighted debt just makes you forget which dice you're using. Either way, the Hollow put everything on the table: coin, contracts, a sealed document with a name no one could read from across the room.

Velchin looked at it all for a long time.

"This doesn't cover it," he said.

"I know," the Hollow said. "But it's everything I have."

Velchin took it anyway. Walked out without another word. The barkeep says his hands were shaking, though he won't say why.

The Hollow sat for another hour. Drank one ale. Left the dice.

You turn them over in your palm. They're warm from the afternoon light. Heavier on one side — loaded to win the game the carrier could never afford to lose. Except he lost it anyway. On purpose, or by accident, or because after long enough it stops mattering which.

A debt is settled. A ledger doesn't close.

The dice sit in your hand, and you find you don't know what to do with them either.

### Idea 2 — *The Return of the Concession of Third Seam* (Epic Fantasy)

The Hall of Accounts is built for ceremonies like this one. The ceiling is vaulted obsidian, carved with every debt acknowledged and discharged by Ironhold since the Second Forming. There is a word in Deep Dwarven for a debt paid in full — *thurvek* — and when it is spoken here, the carvings are said to shift to record it.

You arrive as the ceremony begins. On one side of the long table: Clan-Lord Dorrith Thrice-Stone, in ceremonial iron, who has not set foot in this hall in forty-three years. On the other: Clan-Lord Sevia Emberdawn, still wearing the black of ancestral mourning. Between them: the dicer.

She is not what you expected. Short, weathered, human — a woman in her sixties in traveling clothes that have seen better decades. She holds a sheaf of documents with shaking hands.

Her grandmother won a territorial concession from Ironhold in a game that should never have been played — land already disputed between Thrice-Stone and Emberdawn. For forty years, the concession sat in the hands of an outsider, which meant neither clan could claim it, and neither clan could go to war over it. The dicer is here to give it back.

The ceremony proceeds with perfect solemnity. The documents transfer. *Thurvek* is spoken. The carvings shift.

Dorrith Thrice-Stone turns to Sevia Emberdawn.

The silence that follows is older than either of them.

"So," Dorrith says, very quietly. "It returns to its rightful heirs."

"It was never yours," Sevia says, just as quietly.

The dicer is already walking toward the door. She doesn't look back. She knew, you realize — knew the debt was keeping the peace, and paid it anyway. Because a debt owed is a debt owed, and she didn't have the right to hold it one more day, regardless of what it was holding together.

She paid correctly. She paid with honor.

Behind her, the hall fills with the sound of forty years coming unstuck.

The carvings on the ceiling do not record what happens next.

### Idea 3 — *Four Days at the Table of Sur Deepore* (Gothic)

You find her on the fourth morning: Sur Deepore, matriarch of the Deepore line, sitting in the long shaft of pale light that cuts through the eastern vent of her receiving hall. She is very old. Her hands, when she turns the ivory card over and over, are the color of quarried limestone.

On the table: a set of dice carved from the teeth of something ancient and unnamed. You do not touch them.

The dicer came four days ago. No name given, no name asked. A human, slight and grey-haired, who knocked on the iron door and said only: *I ran the table where your son lost everything. I am here because I owe him an account.*

Sur Deepore let her in.

They played cards for three days. The human lost every hand. The old matriarch is not sure the losses were deliberate, and she has decided it does not matter. What matters is that between hands the dicer spoke of Vorin Deepore the way only someone who watched him play could — how he laughed when he was bluffing, how he could read a room in thirty seconds, how he was better than his losses made him look, the night he walked away from the table and into the mountain and did not come out.

The dicer told Sur Deepore things she had spent forty years not knowing.

On the fourth morning the chair was empty. The dice sat on the table. The card read: *I am sorry. It was my fault. I am glad to have known him.*

Sur Deepore does not look up when you enter. She is still reading it, though she has surely read it a dozen times.

The wound is open. Something old and necessary is draining from it.

The debt is paid. The ledger, closed. This is what closing it looks like: an old woman crying in a shaft of mountain light, holding a dead man's eulogy written twenty years too late, finally given.

The stone remembers.

## Card 11 — The Lawful Guard & the Possessed Shield

> **Card:** A lawful guard wants to drive out a spirit that has possessed a shield, but they will suffer an injury that does not heal.

### Idea 1 — *Evidence, Seal Intact* (Noir)

The shield came in on a Tuesday. A merchant reported that it wouldn't release from his arm and that something inside it had been whispering his wife's name. Constable Wren Aldiss logged it under *Supernatural Disturbance, Object of Unknown Origin*, locked it in the evidence cage, and opened a case file.

She spent three weeks documenting its behavior. She was meticulous — it was why she'd been put on nights, because night shift ran lean and needed people who did not require reminders of procedure.

The spirit spoke to her on the fourteenth night. She wrote down what it said. She did not respond. There was no protocol for engaging an entity in custody, which meant the default was: silence.

On the twenty-first night, she checked the cost of a licensed exorcist. She submitted the funding request. It was denied. Non-essential expenditure.

She drove it out herself on a Wednesday, before the rest of the shift arrived. Section 12C of the supplemental manual: *Entities of Spiritual Origin.* She followed every step. The spirit exited through her left palm anyway, leaving two crossed lines that did not close. When she filed the injury report, she wrote under *Estimated Recovery Time*: *uncertain, ongoing.*

That was eight months ago.

The shield is in the evidence cage. Small red tag: *Case 7741-S. Spirit expelled 09:14. Investigating officer: W. Aldiss.* Someone else says it hums. Aldiss says that's normal — post-expulsion resonance, it fades — and she is not sure this is true, but someone needs to say something official, and she's the one who filed the paperwork.

Her left hand doesn't close all the way on cold mornings. She hasn't amended the report.

The shield is yours if you sign for it. Aldiss watches you take it from the cage. Before you reach the door, she pulls a fresh log sheet and writes your name at the top. Then the date. Then the case number.

Whatever happens next, there will be a record.

### Idea 2 — *The Ward Before Words* (Mythic Fantasy)

The shield came up from the old layers during roadwork — the third such artifact that week, which meant Sergeant Marek Voln did not call a priest. The last time he'd called a priest, the man had fainted in the presence of the second thing from the old layers, and the subsequent paperwork took the rest of the shift.

He set the shield on the table in the ward house and stood watch over it. This was what he knew how to do: stand present, stand quiet, don't leave the sector exposed.

The spirit rose at midnight. Not dramatically — it lifted from the iron boss like heat from a stone. It had no shape Voln could name, but it had weight, and weight in the middle of a room was a kind of claim, and claims he knew how to address.

"I am Sergeant Marek Voln. Warden of the Copper Market District. You are within my sector."

The spirit said something that did not resolve into language.

"I cannot have you loose in this ward. There are families here. A bakery that opens before dawn. I need you to return to where you came from, or to depart entirely."

The spirit said something else.

"I understand you predate this law," Voln said. "I'm not invoking my law. I'm invoking my sector. This is where I stand."

He could not have said how long they remained like that — the sergeant and the thing older than his city, possibly older than the concept of cities. Eventually the spirit moved. Not back into the shield. Through the wall, or through the concept of walls; Voln was not certain the distinction held.

He discovered the wound when he sat down: two burns at the collar lines of his coat, shaped like a grip. They did not blister. They did not fade. He pressed two fingers against each and thought about the bakery opening before dawn.

He had stopped the immediate threat. He was less certain what that meant. He didn't know if "gone" meant the same thing to the spirit that it meant to him, and he suspected it didn't, and he suspected that was a question he was now going to carry in the place where his collar sat against his skin for the rest of his career.

The shield stayed on the table. He didn't file a report. He wasn't sure what category to use.

In the morning, the laborer who'd dug it up asked if he could have it back.

"No," Voln said.

### Idea 3 — *Constable Hatch and the Morning Market* (Cozy Fantasy)

Constable Perrin Hatch was the sort of officer the Willowmere market had named a cheese after. Not a serious cheese — a small, semi-soft one, good for children, sold at the dairy cart on Thursdays. She had been on day watch for eleven years and knew which fishmonger hid bad crates under good ones, which baker's daughter snuck out after curfew, and which children's names to learn first because those were the ones most likely to be trouble before noon.

The shield arrived on market day. It drifted through a dozen hands before anyone admitted they didn't want it. By the time it reached Perrin, the iron was warm in a way that had nothing to do with the sun.

She smiled at the spice vendor who'd passed it off, told him everything was fine, and walked it back to the watch house tucked under her arm like a loaf of bread.

She drove out the spirit that night. The watch manual's section on spiritual entities was vague to the point of uselessness, so she improvised from first principles: clarity, presence, authority, intent. She stood in the middle of the watch house and told the spirit with firm politeness that it needed to go, because the market opened at six and she wasn't going to spend her morning on this.

The spirit went. She was fairly sure.

Her right knee locked when she tried to stand. She straightened it manually, wincing once, and filed the shield on the evidence shelf — second row, between a confiscated knife and a jar of something the herbalist had labeled *regulatory* — and wrote: *Shield, iron, decorated. Spirit expelled per manual sec. 9. Injury sustained: right knee, persistent. —P. Hatch.*

In the morning she walked to the market and greeted twenty-three people before the first stall opened. No one asked about the limp. She didn't mention it.

The knee doesn't improve.

When you come to Willowmere the third time, Perrin takes the shield from its shelf herself and sets it in your hands. It hums at a frequency that isn't quite sound. She tells you it's fine, that it gave them a bit of trouble but everything's sorted now.

She's smiling when she says it. She will be paying for it for some time yet, and she isn't going to tell anyone, because it's her market, and it's her sector, and that's what the job is.
