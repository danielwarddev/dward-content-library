# Solo Consulting Playbook for a .NET Expert

## Objective

Build a side consulting business around specific, valuable outcomes for .NET teams. Start with a small fixed-scope engagement, use it to discover repeated customer problems, and gradually turn the resulting methods and assets into workshops, toolkits, licensed products, or software.

The goal is not to sell generic programming labor. It is to package specialized expertise into an offer that is easy for an engineering leader to understand, approve, and purchase.

## Existing Advantages

- Deep experience in .NET, AI, and software testing
- Recognition as a Microsoft MVP in .NET
- An established body of technical writing at [daninacan.com](https://daninacan.com)
- Existing relationships in the .NET and MVP communities
- Credibility with developers and engineering leaders

These are meaningful advantages. The main challenge is not proving technical competence; it is connecting that expertise to a specific business problem and making the next step obvious to a potential customer.

## Recommended Business Progression

Use consulting as the first stage of a broader product business:

1. **Consulting:** Solve a narrowly defined problem manually and learn what customers value.
2. **Productized service:** Standardize the scope, process, deliverables, timeline, and price.
3. **Reusable product:** Package repeated artifacts as templates, tools, training, or licensed intellectual property.
4. **SaaS:** Automate a recurring problem only after multiple customers demonstrate that it is worth paying to solve repeatedly.

This progression produces revenue and market evidence before requiring a large product investment.

## Protect the Day Job First

Before accepting outside work, review all applicable employment policies and agreements for:

- Moonlighting or outside-employment restrictions
- Non-compete and non-solicitation terms
- Conflict-of-interest requirements
- Intellectual-property ownership provisions
- Requirements to disclose or obtain approval for outside work
- Restrictions involving current, former, or prospective employer clients

Do not approach the consulting company's clients, reuse its confidential information or materials, perform outside work on company time or equipment, or offer work that violates an applicable restriction. Obtain written approval if the agreement or company policy requires it. Consult a qualified attorney if the terms are unclear or consequential.

## Sell an Outcome, Not “Consulting”

Avoid advertising only that general .NET consulting is available. That forces a buyer to identify a project, define the engagement, estimate its risk, and decide how to use the consultant.

Instead, sell a fixed-scope diagnostic or assessment with:

- A clearly identified customer
- A recognizable and costly problem
- Defined inputs and deliverables
- A predictable timeline
- Explicit scope and exclusions
- A fixed or starting price

Diagnostics are especially suitable for a first engagement because they are bounded, carry relatively little buyer risk, can fit around a full-time job, and can lead to implementation or training work.

## Potential Productized Offers

Choose one offer initially rather than launching a menu of services.

| Offer | Deliverable | Illustrative starting price |
|---|---|---:|
| AI Readiness Review for .NET Teams | Architecture review, use-case shortlist, security and testing risks, implementation roadmap | $2,500–$7,500 |
| .NET Test Suite Health Check | Flakiness, execution-time, coverage, CI, and maintainability analysis with prioritized recommendations | $2,000–$5,000 |
| .NET Architecture Review | Codebase assessment, dependency and deployment risks, written plan, and recorded or live walkthrough | $3,000–$8,000 |
| Legacy .NET Modernization Assessment | Upgrade inventory, blockers, effort estimates, and phased migration plan | $4,000–$10,000 |
| Private Team Workshop | One or two days on AI-enabled .NET, testing, architecture, or modernization | $3,000–$10,000 |

These prices are starting hypotheses, not universal market rates. Scope, customer size, risk, access requirements, and demonstrated demand should determine the final price.

## Recommended First Offer

### .NET Test Suite Health Check

> I review a .NET application's automated tests and CI results, identify the most important reliability, performance, and maintainability problems, and provide a prioritized remediation plan.

An initial pilot could include:

- One repository or application
- Review of test organization and representative test code
- Review of available CI test history
- Identification of flaky, slow, brittle, or unnecessarily costly tests
- Assessment of test isolation, fixtures, data management, and integration boundaries
- A written report with prioritized recommendations
- A 60-minute findings walkthrough
- Delivery within two weeks
- A fixed pilot price, potentially around $1,500 for a deliberately limited initial scope

The pilot should be smaller than the eventual standard engagement, not an unlimited engagement sold cheaply. State exclusions such as implementation, production support, security certification, or review of additional repositories.

## Positioning

A possible broad positioning statement is:

> I help established .NET teams adopt AI and modernize their engineering practices without compromising reliability.

For the first offer, use more specific language:

> I help .NET teams identify why their automated tests are slow, flaky, or difficult to maintain and give them a prioritized plan to restore confidence in CI.

Specific positioning makes it easier for the right customer to recognize the problem and refer someone else who has it.

## Finding Customer #1

The first customer is most likely to come through an existing professional relationship or a trusted introduction—not an anonymous mass-marketing campaign.

### Build a List of 25 People

List people rather than companies:

- Former coworkers and managers
- Microsoft MVP peers
- Conference and user-group organizers
- People met at technical events
- Blog readers who have sent questions or feedback
- Open-source maintainers and contributors
- Developer-tool vendors serving .NET teams
- Engineering leaders who have engaged with relevant posts
- Friends working on .NET teams

Rank the list:

1. Five people who know and trust the technical work
2. Ten people adjacent to engineering managers, directors, architects, or CTOs
3. Ten weaker but relevant professional connections

The purpose is not to pressure 25 contacts into buying. It is to find teams experiencing the problem and obtain relevant introductions.

### Outreach Cadence

Send approximately five thoughtful, individual messages each week. Personal, specific messages are preferable to a generic public announcement.

Track:

- Person contacted
- Relevant relationship or context
- Date contacted
- Response
- Problem evidence learned
- Introduction offered
- Follow-up date
- Resulting conversation or proposal

### Message to Someone Known Well

> Hey [Name]—I'm piloting a small, fixed-scope service for .NET teams struggling with slow or unreliable automated tests. I review their test suite and CI results and deliver a prioritized remediation plan.
>
> I'm looking for one team to work with outside my normal employment. Does that problem sound familiar at your company? If not, is there an engineering manager in your network whom I should speak with?

### Message to a Community Contact

> Hi [Name]—we met through [specific context]. I've been writing and speaking about .NET testing for some time, and I'm now piloting a fixed-scope test-suite health check.
>
> I'm not asking you to buy anything, but I'd value your perspective: do you see teams struggling enough with flaky or slow tests to purchase an independent assessment? If someone comes to mind, would you be comfortable introducing us?

### Message to Someone Who Engaged With Relevant Content

> Thanks for your comment about [specific issue]. I'm researching how .NET teams handle this in production and am offering a small test-suite assessment to a few teams. Is this something your team actively struggles with?

These messages are invitations to a relevant professional conversation, not high-pressure sales pitches.

## Introvert-Friendly Customer Acquisition

Most of this process can be asynchronous:

- Publish technical articles
- Add calls to action to existing articles
- Send individual emails or direct messages
- Use a written intake form
- Hold only short qualification calls
- Deliver findings through a written report and recorded presentation when appropriate
- Provide support by email within defined boundaries

Introversion is not a consulting disadvantage. Effective consulting sales rely heavily on preparation, thoughtful questions, careful listening, and clear written communication rather than charisma or constant networking.

## Turn the Blog Into a Consulting Channel

Add a relevant call to action to high-traffic or closely related articles:

> **Need help with unreliable or slow .NET tests?**  
> I offer a fixed-scope test-suite health check for .NET teams. You'll receive an independent assessment and prioritized remediation plan. [See the details / contact me.]

Create a dedicated service page containing:

- **Who it is for:** For example, engineering managers responsible for a substantial .NET application
- **Symptoms:** Flaky CI, slow suites, low confidence, brittle fixtures, or expensive test maintenance
- **Business consequences:** Delayed releases, ignored failures, wasted engineering time, and increased production risk
- **What the engagement includes:** The investigation and review process
- **What the customer receives:** Report, prioritized plan, and walkthrough
- **Scope boundaries:** Repository count, available CI history, implementation exclusions, and support limits
- **Timeline:** For example, two weeks after access and intake are complete
- **Price:** A fixed pilot price or a clear starting price
- **Credibility:** Microsoft MVP status, relevant experience, articles, talks, and eventual testimonials
- **Next action:** A 20–30 minute qualification call or a concise intake form

The blog demonstrates expertise. The service page makes it clear that the expertise can be purchased.

## Qualification Call Structure

A sales conversation should be a diagnosis, not a long presentation. A 25-minute structure is sufficient:

1. **Context:** “Tell me about the application and the team.”
2. **Problem:** “What is currently frustrating you about the tests?”
3. **Impact:** “What happens when CI is slow or unreliable?”
4. **Evidence:** “How frequently does this occur, and how do you measure it?”
5. **Previous attempts:** “What have you already tried?”
6. **Urgency:** “Why address this now rather than six months from now?”
7. **Desired outcome:** “What would meaningfully improve after this engagement?”
8. **Decision process:** “Who else would need to approve an assessment?”
9. **Fit:** Briefly explain the fixed-scope offer and how it maps to the stated problem.
10. **Next step:** Agree to a proposal, another conversation with stakeholders, or that the opportunity is not a fit.

Ask follow-up questions and take notes. Use the prospect's own language in the proposal.

Do not perform the entire diagnosis for free. Establish that the problem is understood and explain the assessment process, but reserve detailed investigation and recommendations for the paid engagement.

## One-Page Proposal Structure

Keep the proposal concise:

1. **Current situation:** Summarize the customer's problem in their language.
2. **Desired outcome:** State what the engagement is intended to achieve.
3. **Scope:** List the included systems, repositories, evidence, and activities.
4. **Deliverables:** Specify the report, findings, roadmap, meeting, or recording.
5. **Exclusions:** State what is not included.
6. **Timeline:** Include start conditions, delivery dates, and customer dependencies.
7. **Price and payment:** State the fixed fee, payment schedule, and applicable taxes or expenses.
8. **Customer responsibilities:** Access, points of contact, available CI history, and timely responses.
9. **Confidentiality and intellectual property:** Align these terms with the contract.
10. **Validity:** Add an expiration date to the proposal.

For a small engagement, consider either full payment in advance or 50% at booking and 50% upon delivery. Use a written agreement and a professional invoice. Obtain appropriate legal and accounting advice for contracts, business structure, insurance, taxes, and data handling.

## Delivery Process

A repeatable delivery workflow might be:

1. Signed agreement and initial payment
2. Intake questionnaire
3. Access and security requirements confirmed
4. Repository and CI evidence collected
5. Automated and manual analysis performed
6. Findings ranked by impact, confidence, and effort
7. Written report prepared
8. Findings walkthrough delivered
9. Questions handled within a defined follow-up window
10. Final payment collected, if applicable
11. Feedback, testimonial, case-study permission, and introduction requested

Do not accept sensitive access casually. Define how source code, credentials, logs, customer data, and generated artifacts will be accessed, stored, transmitted, retained, and deleted.

## Turn Customer #1 Into Customer #2

After delivering useful work, ask directly:

> Was this valuable enough that you would recommend it to another engineering leader? If so, may I use a short testimonial, write an anonymized case study, and ask you for one introduction?

A good case study should describe:

- The customer's original symptoms
- The operational or business impact
- What the assessment found
- Which recommendations the team adopted
- Any measurable improvements
- A customer quotation, when permitted

Never reveal customer details or confidential findings without explicit permission.

## Productizing Repeated Work

After each engagement, identify reusable assets:

- Intake questionnaires
- Assessment checklists and scorecards
- Roslyn analyzers
- Test-quality and dependency-reporting tools
- Architecture review templates
- GitHub Actions workflows
- Reference applications
- AI prompt and evaluation suites
- Migration scripts
- Report templates
- Prioritization frameworks
- Workshop exercises

Standardize these internally first to improve delivery speed and consistency. Later, selected assets can become paid products such as:

- **Production-Ready .NET AI Starter Kit:** Evaluation harness, tracing, resilience, security checks, and reference architecture
- **Reliable Integration Testing Kit for ASP.NET Core:** Containers, fixtures, CI configuration, test data patterns, and documentation
- **.NET Modernization Assessment Toolkit:** Analyzers, inventories, scorecards, and report generation
- **Engineering Standards Pack for .NET Teams:** Analyzers, `.editorconfig`, architecture tests, CI gates, and adoption guidance

Sell to teams where possible. Team or company licensing can create more value than pricing only for individual developers.

## Education as a Distribution and Revenue Channel

Broad beginner courses are crowded. Focus education on narrow, expensive outcomes informed by real customer work:

- Testing non-deterministic AI features in .NET
- Shipping a production-ready .NET AI feature
- Modernizing a legacy .NET application without a rewrite
- Eliminating flaky ASP.NET Core integration tests
- Architecture testing for large .NET codebases

Start with a live cohort or private corporate workshop rather than immediately recording a large self-paced course. A live version validates willingness to pay and reveals the parts that need better explanation. Record and productize it only after the material works with real participants.

## Additional Consulting-Adjacent Revenue

### Technical Training Retainers

Offer a monthly architecture clinic, engineering office hour, code-review session, or internal learning session. This can create recurring revenue without requiring a full software product.

### Licensing

License workshops, assessment methods, starter kits, standards, or internal training materials by team or company.

### Sponsorships

Relevant developer-tool companies may sponsor blog posts, newsletters, talks, sample repositories, or educational series. Protect credibility through strict relevance, editorial independence, and clear disclosure.

### Referral Partnerships

An assessment may reveal implementation work that exceeds available time. A transparent relationship with a trusted consultancy or specialist can provide an implementation path. Avoid undisclosed incentives or arrangements that could bias recommendations.

## 90-Day Plan

### Weeks 1–2: Define and Publish One Offer

- Review employment restrictions and obtain any required approval
- Select one buyer and one painful problem
- Define scope, exclusions, deliverables, timeline, and pilot price
- Create a basic service page
- Add calls to action to relevant blog posts
- Identify 25 professional contacts
- Conduct problem interviews where possible

Interview questions should focus on actual behavior:

- “Tell me about the last time this happened.”
- “What did it delay or cost?”
- “How are you handling it now?”
- “Who owns the problem?”
- “What have you already tried?”
- “Has budget ever been allocated to fix it?”

Avoid relying on hypothetical questions such as “Would you buy this?”

### Weeks 3–6: Sell Three Pilot Engagements

- Contact approximately five relevant people each week
- Ask for problem evidence and introductions
- Hold short qualification conversations
- Refine the offer language using the terminology prospects use
- Send concise proposals to qualified prospects
- Keep pilot work bounded and paid

An initial funnel target could be:

> 20 thoughtful messages → 5 relevant conversations → 2 proposals → 1 customer

This is a planning model, not a guaranteed conversion rate.

### Weeks 7–10: Standardize Delivery

- Improve the intake process
- Automate repetitive analysis
- Refine the report and recommendation structure
- Document the delivery workflow
- Identify which findings recur across engagements
- Collect testimonials and case-study evidence
- Raise the price when the process and value are better understood

### Weeks 11–12: Create a Distribution Loop

For each repeated customer problem:

1. Publish a useful article addressing part of it.
2. Offer a relevant checklist or small tool in exchange for an email signup.
3. Direct qualified readers to the fixed-scope service.
4. Convert repeated service work into a paid toolkit or workshop.
5. Consider software only when recurring demand is evident.

## Four-Week Customer #1 Sprint

| Week | Primary action |
|---|---|
| 1 | Review employment restrictions, define one offer, and publish a simple service page |
| 2 | Contact five strong relationships and five community contacts individually |
| 3 | Hold 3–5 research or qualification conversations and refine the offer |
| 4 | Send 1–2 paid pilot proposals and continue contacting five relevant people per week |

Measure conversations, proposals, paid engagements, and referrals—not impressions or follower counts.

## Example Long-Term Revenue Mix

A diversified side business might eventually include:

- One $4,000 assessment each month
- One $5,000–$8,000 private workshop each quarter
- A $199–$499 toolkit selling several copies per month
- Carefully selected sponsorship or affiliate revenue
- A recurring training or advisory retainer
- Eventually, a B2B SaaS product based on a problem validated through paid work

This is an illustrative model rather than an income forecast. Available time, employment restrictions, taxes, delivery capacity, lead flow, and market demand will determine what is realistic.
